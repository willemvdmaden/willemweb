The evidence behind the taxonomy: a one-page summary

The most relevant studies from a related-work update covering February to September 2026 (113 papers with a user study or conceptual contribution; 64 purely computational evaluations), by dimension, followed by the topics that cut across dimensions. Optional reading.

Surface

Friedman et al. (arXiv, March) manipulate response length against reasoning correctness and find a middle length best for error detection. Li, Song, Sundaram and Karahalios (CHI) show a less verbose tutoring chatbot improves learners' detection of logical errors. Wu, Jobanputra and Demberg et al. (HAXD, N=304) cross supporting detail with confidence tone and measure belief switching. Garcia Valencia et al. (Frontiers in AI) quantify the readability versus accuracy trade-off when explanations are prompted to an eighth-grade level. Computationally, Wu et al. (ICLR) show an inverted U between chain-of-thought length and accuracy.

Epistemic stance and Interpersonal stance

The memo files these together as "Relational", by far its largest category.

Cheng, Yu, Lee et al. (Science) show sycophantic AI is preferred, rated more trustworthy, and reduces prosocial intentions across 2,405 participants; Ibrahim, Hafner and Rocher (Nature) show that training for warmth raises error rates and validation of false beliefs. Villavicencio, Pan and Wang (FAccT) add a granularity dimension to expressed confidence, from response level to claim level. Sun, Wei, Bosch et al. (CHI EA) manipulate certainty framing, correctness and timing of rationales in one design, and find certainty cues raise trust while timing does nothing. Denecke and Gabarron (Frontiers in AI) separate confident tone from disclaimers in medical advice. Braca et al. (IUI) show persuasive rhetoric embedded in LLM explanations raises compliance without users noticing. A cluster of CHI papers decomposes sycophancy into styles such as praise, agreement, self-deprecation and stance adaptation.

Structural

Fernandes, Buschek, Tankelevitch et al. (arXiv, N=559) compare full reasoning traces, summary traces and answers only, and find summaries keep performance while raising trust and appeal. Spitzer et al. (npj Digital Medicine, 2,020 radiologist assessments) compare formats of LLM diagnostic explanations. Pang et al. and Zhou et al. (both IUI) restructure chain-of-thought into navigable hierarchies and interactive step views. Palod, Biswas and Kambhampati (arXiv) test whether traces, summaries and post-hoc explanations help users catch wrong answers or simply persuade. Qian et al. (Findings of ACL) make conclusion-first versus reasoning-first ordering a design variable.

Delivery

Pareek et al. (CHI) describe a just-right level of process visibility that shifts with task and user. Kirmayr et al. (CHI, N=45) show intermediate progress and reasoning feedback from an in-car voice agent raises trust and lowers load. Moon, Huang and Xiao (CHI EA) find editable chain-of-thought raises perceived control while read-only chain-of-thought raises inappropriate reliance. Srinivasan and Thomason (IUI) time supporting versus counter-explanations to the user's current trust and cut inappropriate reliance by up to 38 percent. Ojewale et al. (CHB: AH, N=495) combine verbal hedging with visual confidence indicators and find the combination fragile.

Source

Warren, Sun, Shklovski et al. (FAccT) show users fall back on underlying evidence, and use it less when natural-language explanations look sufficient. Knor et al. (CHB Reports, N=1,417) run a conjoint on cited sources and verification disclaimers. Sun, Warren, Shklovski et al. (ACL) generate explanations of why the model is uncertain, tied to conflicting evidence. Schreieder, Schopf and Färber (ACL) give a unified taxonomy of attribution, citation and quotation. Herrewijnen et al. (arXiv) propose actionability as a criterion alongside plausibility and faithfulness. Computationally, Li, Yang, Hazarika et al. (arXiv) show visible traces and latent answer commitment align on only about 62 percent of steps.

Semantic

Teng et al. (npj Digital Medicine, randomized, N=111) find misleading explanations hurt novices' diagnostic accuracy while correct explanations do not help. Palta, Rankel and Wiegreffe (ACL, 3,000 human judgments) show LLM rationales move human plausibility ratings in whichever direction they argue. Yang, Kagawa and Baba (FAccT, N=167) show explanations aligned to a user's own decision criteria steer decisions even when inaccurate. Kenaston et al. (npj Digital Medicine) provide a clinician-validated taxonomy of reasoning errors.

Topics that cut across dimensions

  • Visible reasoning traces. The largest cross-cutting theme. The 2026 work splits it into what form the trace takes (full, summarized, hierarchical, editable, conclusion-first: Structural), whether and when it is shown (hidden, on request, before or alongside the answer: Delivery), and whether it reflects the computation (Source). The strongest result is that summaries and read-only traces raise trust and agreement without improving accuracy, which several papers frame as false trust.
  • Sycophancy as a property. Eighteen papers treat agreement, flattery, face-saving and stance adaptation as manipulable dimensions of a response, with evidence from Science and Nature that users prefer the agreeable variants and that warmth trades off against correctness.
  • Persuasion and personalization. Rhetorical strategies, framing, alignment with the user's decision criteria, contextualization and personality-aligned wording all move trust or decisions, and users rarely notice. This sits between the stance dimensions and Semantic.
  • Confidence expression. Has moved from presence to structure: granularity (per claim), modality (verbal plus visual), explaining why the model is uncertain, multi-turn recalibration, and the faithfulness of hedges themselves.
