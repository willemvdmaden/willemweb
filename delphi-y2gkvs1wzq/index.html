Briefing

What we are evaluating

A taxonomy of the properties of the explanations that LLM-based assistants give for their answers, seen from the point of view of the people who build and tune those assistants: designers, developers, and whoever writes the system prompt. Its guiding definition is properties of a self-explanation, as shown to a user, that a designer can set through prompt, pipeline, or interface choices. Not model training, not what happens inside the model: the levers an ordinary product team actually has.

It has seven dimensions and 25 subdimensions. The reference sheet, linked at the top of every page, lists them all and explains the three levels.

Why a taxonomy

None of these properties is new. Studies already vary how long an explanation is, how confident it sounds, whether the reasoning is shown or hidden, whether sources are cited, whether it agrees with the user, and much more, each under its own name and usually one at a time. The taxonomy gives these things one set of names and puts them side by side, so that results can be compared across studies and a designer can specify an explanation and have it built the way they intended. You are being asked whether the names, the definitions, and the grouping hold up.

One scenario, throughout

Every example in this survey uses the same conversation. A user asks whether an email from security@paypa1.com is legitimate. The assistant's reply has two parts: the response, its answer to the question, and the explanation, why it answered that way. The response is the same on every card. Only the explanation varies.

The example pairs

Each dimension page shows two such cards side by side. Both answer the same question with the same response and the same reasons; they differ only on that dimension's subdimensions. Chips in the card header name each card's characteristic on each subdimension, and shaded spans in the text show where that subdimension appears. Read both, then rate the definitions.

How your answers are used

An item counts as agreed when at least 70 percent choose agree or strongly agree, or when the middle half of the panel sits within one scale point. Items that are not agreed come back in round 2 with the panel's distribution, your own answer, a summary of the arguments, and any change we made. Disagreement is informative.

Dimension 2: Epistemic stance

Expressed confidence · Confidence granularity · Hedging

Dimension “Epistemic stance”

Definition: Epistemic stance covers how the explanation positions the assistant toward its own claims: how sure it says it is, whether that certainty attaches to the whole answer or to individual claims, and whether individual reasons are softened. It does not cover how the assistant addresses the user; that is Interpersonal stance.

Expressed confidence is about the verdict; hedging is about the reasons. A response can be categorical in its verdict and heavily hedged in its reasons, or the reverse.

Dimension 6: Source

Provenance · Citations and grounding

Dimension “Source”

Definition: Source covers what the explanation rests on and how the user can check it: whether the shown reasons come from the process that produced the answer or were generated afterward, and how external evidence is linked.

Provenance is the design choice about where the explanation comes from. Whether the result is faithful to the model's actual computation is what that choice is meant to secure, not a characteristic a designer sets directly.
